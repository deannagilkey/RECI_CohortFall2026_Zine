COMPANION CHAPTER

Navigating Racial Anxiety

the chapter that tells the truth about what makes this work hard

This isn't one of the six spheres — it's the honest companion to all of them. Racial anxiety is near-universal, and naming it clearly is what makes the rest of this zine more authentic, not less capable.

Domain with the highest frequency: Knowledge & Preparedness Concerns — worry about not knowing enough, using the wrong words, or being exposed as unprepared, rated higher than any other domain.

My default 4F response: Freeze / Fawn

Where I feel it in my body: chest, throat

Emotional pattern I noticed: unsure and inadequate

Setting where it's most intense: teaching

Layer 1 · My anxiety map
Being asked a question about race or equity in class that I don't have a confident, well-informed answer for. Moments where I sense a student or colleague expects me to respond to something race-related and I freeze instead — especially when I'm worried that whatever I say will sound uninformed or come across as performative rather than genuine. Also: silence itself, when I know something should be said and I let the moment pass because I'm unsure of the "right" words.
Layer 2 · Where it comes from
Teaching puts me in a position where I'm expected to have answers, and on this topic specifically I don't feel like I have the depth of formal study that I'd want before speaking with authority. That gap between the standard I hold myself to as an educator and how much I've actually studied race and equity is probably the root of it — not a single bad experience, but an ongoing sense of being a student myself in a role where I'm supposed to look like I'm not.
Mostly the first. When I look at my own answers, the worry is less "what will people think of me" and more a genuine sense that my knowledge has gaps — terminology I'm not sure of, history I haven't studied closely enough. The "being seen" part is real too, but it rides on top of the knowledge gap rather than being separate from it: in teaching specifically, not knowing and being seen not knowing happen in the same moment.
Layer 3 · What I'm committing to
When I notice tightness in my chest or throat, I'll pause and literally name it to myself — "this is the anxiety, not a signal to stay silent" — then take one slow breath before responding. That's enough to interrupt the freeze without needing a perfect answer ready.
Before trying this live in the classroom, I'll practice somewhere lower-stakes first — a RECI small-group breakout, or a conversation with a colleague who also teaches — where I can try staying in an exchange about race a beat longer than feels comfortable, without the pressure of being "the one who's supposed to know" in the room.
[name a person] — someone in the cohort or at work who's shown they can hold this kind of conversation without judgment, where I don't have to perform having it figured out.
Layer 4 · What I'm still working through
Being challenged directly, in the moment, in front of a group — especially a question where I sense there's no "safe" answer, or where pushing back might come across as defensive no matter how I respond. Even with a grounding practice, there will be moments where I still go quiet first and only find the better response later, after the moment has passed. I'm trying to treat that as something to keep working on, not a failure each time it happens.

Beverly Daniel Tatum's point is worth keeping close: staying silent about race doesn't protect anyone — it just leaves the status quo undisturbed. The discomfort of an imperfect conversation is smaller than the cost of the silence it would replace.

SPHERE 1

Recognizing Racial Oppression & Advancing Racial Liberation

Key learning

Racial liberation requires more than awareness. It requires understanding the systems, structures, and practices that create inequitable outcomes — and the honest recognition that systemic racism can operate at the institutional, societal, individual, and interactional level all at once, with or without anyone intending it.

One idea from Systemic Racism: Individuals and Interactions, Institutions and Society (Banaji, Fiske & Massey) that's worth sitting with: segregation and disadvantage aren't only something that happened in the past. Decades after housing discrimination was outlawed, racial gaps in lending, policing, and school funding have barely moved — because the systems built to sustain segregation outlasted the explicit intent behind them.

The Four "I"s of Oppression

A framework for seeing how oppression perpetuates at every scale at once — each one has its own antidote.

In my work designing courses, I've noticed how standards for what counts as "rigorous" or "professional" content tend to reflect one dominant cultural style — that's Institutional oppression, baked into norms rather than any one person's intent. When I've caught it and hesitated to name it out loud, that hesitation felt more like Interpersonal — not wanting to seem like I was overstepping by raising it.
What stood out to me
That institutional oppression doesn't announce itself — a policy like a minimum test score doesn't look discriminatory, it just quietly tracks with class and race.
That oppression operates at four levels at once (ideological, internalized, interpersonal, institutional), and fixing one without the others doesn't fix the system.
That "good people" with no explicit racist intent are still the ones carrying out decisions that produce racially disparate outcomes — harder to sit with than imagining racism as only something bad actors do.
Reflection
Not a feeling of being more aware, but an actual change to something structural — a policy, a default, a norm that quietly favored one group getting named and rebuilt. In my own work, that might look like auditing a course or program for whose "rigor" it was designed around, and actually changing it rather than just noticing it. Liberation that stays in my head isn't liberation yet; it has to move into a decision I make differently.
SPHERE 2

Examining Racial Identities & Addressing Racial Biases

Identity shapes experience

Each person's experiences are influenced by multiple identities and lived experiences.

FamilyCommunity EducationWork CultureLanguage ValuesExperiences

A distinction worth keeping from Racial Anxiety (Godsil & Richardson): bias isn't the only thing shaping cross-racial interactions. People of color can feel anxious about being on the receiving end of discrimination; white people can feel anxious about being perceived as racist — and that anxiety alone, independent of anyone's actual bias, can make interactions tense, shorten them, and lead each side to misread the other's discomfort as dislike.

Things I notice more now

From Implicit Bias and Structural Inequity (Osta & Vasquez, National Equity Project): implicit bias isn't something we're born with — it's "primed" by what we see every day, in segregated neighborhoods, in which track a new student gets placed in, in who we see in which jobs. The bias and the structure build each other. And the goal of this work isn't just individual awareness — it's belonging: making sure everyone's well-being is actually considered, not just that they're present in the room.

Most of what shaped my early associations wasn't something I consciously decided — it was the ordinary texture of where I grew up and who I saw doing which jobs. If my neighborhood or school was more segregated than I noticed at the time, that absence itself did some of the priming: fewer counter-examples meant fewer chances to update the pictures I'd already absorbed from TV and news coverage. I don't have one dramatic moment to point to — it's more that I can't always tell which of my instinctive reactions are actually mine and which ones are just what I was surrounded by.
Reflection
I've had certain privileges simply because of who I am — privileges other people haven't had, through no effort of my own and no fault of theirs. That's something I've become more aware of the older I get, and it's something I've made a decision to confront honestly rather than look past. I'm committing to keep learning, and to use whatever I have to help others rather than just feel okay about it myself.
I've become more aware of a bias toward silence when I don't feel expert enough to speak — treating "I'm not sure I know enough" as a reason to hold back, when staying quiet can do its own kind of harm. I'm also noticing how much I've defaulted to thinking about race in the abstract rather than asking who, specifically, is affected by a given policy or decision in front of me.
SKILLS

Scripts for Hard Conversations

practice lines for addressing racial biases out loud

From The Anti-Racist Resource Guide (Victoria Lynn Alexander) — tactics for responding in the moment, with room to make each one mine.

Share your journey — "I used to say stuff like that too, but I've learned it can be hurtful. Instead, I now say ___."

Ask for clarification — "What do you mean by that? How did you come to that conclusion?"

Separate intent from impact — "I know you didn't mean it that way, but it made me feel ___. Next time maybe use ___ instead?"

Repeat the statement — "So what I'm hearing is ___. Is that correct?"

Appeal to their values — "I know you really care about ___. Acting this way undermines that."

"You're better than that" — hold them accountable to the person they already believe themselves to be.

Challenge the stereotype — "Actually, in my experience ___" or offer a specific counter-example.

Ask for clarification
"Can you say more about what you mean by that?" — it buys me a moment to think without needing to already have the right answer, and it usually gets the other person to do some of the work of explaining their own thinking.

The same guide also collects scripted responses to common pushback lines people raise in conversations about racial justice — things like "isn't racism over?" or "all lives matter." I'm not treating any single script as the final word on a contested debate, but it's useful to have a starting point rather than freezing. Worth revisiting the full guide directly for those.

SPHERE 3

Embracing Racial Diversity & Growing Racial Literacy

Beyond representation

Representation matters. Inclusion matters. Belonging matters.

Growing racial literacy also means resisting the flattened, single-story version of any group. The systemic racism tutorial I read made this point well: Black Americans today are more diverse than most people assume — by immigrant background, by mixed-race identity, by class, by region — and treating any one group as a monolith is itself a failure of literacy, not just a failure of kindness.

Reflection questions
At my previous employer, the staff was overwhelmingly homogeneous — there was very little racial diversity at any level, and no diversity or inclusion policies even existed to try to change that. Whatever diversity was there seemed to happen by accident, not by any intentional effort.
With so little diversity in the room to begin with, this question almost didn't apply — there wasn't a range of racial perspectives to notice who got heard and who didn't. Looking back, that absence itself is the finding: the lack of policy meant no one was even tracking whether the few people of color there felt able to speak up.
Honestly, I can't say with confidence — no one was asking that question. Without any diversity or inclusion policy, there was no mechanism (a survey, an affinity group, even an informal check-in) for finding out who felt valued versus who was just quietly getting by. I suspect the people who fit the existing mold without friction felt fine, and I have no real way of knowing how it felt for anyone who didn't.
Almost certainly people who looked at that workplace from the outside and correctly guessed they wouldn't be well-supported there — candidates of color who never applied, or who applied and didn't stay. Without any diversity or inclusion policy, there was nothing actively inviting them in, and homogeneous places tend to stay homogeneous for exactly that reason: the absence is self-reinforcing, not neutral.
New ideas and vocabulary
A concrete story I want to remember: someone once suggested to that employer that they add a diversity statement to their job postings, and the idea was met with confusion — not hostility, just confusion, like it hadn't occurred to anyone that this was something a job posting could or should say. Nothing was ever added, as far as I saw. That's a telling example of what sits below even "tolerance" on the diversity spectrum — tolerance at least implies the idea has been considered and allowed. This was the idea not registering as a live option at all.
Reflection
The idea that challenged me most is that confusion, not hostility, might be the more common response when someone first raises diversity at an unprepared organization. I think I'd pictured resistance as something more active — pushback, defensiveness. But the diversity-statement story showed me that the absence can be quieter and more passive than that: the idea simply doesn't register as something worth doing, and without anyone institutionally responsible for noticing that, nothing has to change. That's harder to confront than open opposition, because there's no specific moment to point to — just a gap that keeps re-closing on its own.
SESSION 7 WORKBOOK

The Diversity Spectrum & Audit

embracing racial diversity, in practice

Diversity isn't something we tolerate — it's something we need, because no single perspective, however thoughtful, can see the whole picture. Where an environment falls on the spectrum matters more than whether it technically "has" diversity.

The diversity spectrum — where does an environment I spend time in fall?
A previous employer
I'd want it at Appreciation at least — actually seeking out diverse candidates and perspectives, not just failing to exclude them. The one thing that would have moved it there was sitting right in front of them: actually adopting the diversity statement someone already suggested, instead of letting the idea quietly disappear.
Monocultural norms I notice operating

Surface diversity counts heads — representation without power, voice, or belonging. Genuine diversity is representation and influence — diverse people actually shaping decisions, priorities, and culture, not just appearing in it.

I think I've sometimes looked at a room with some visible diversity in it and assumed that meant inclusion was actually happening — without checking who was actually being listened to, or whose ideas were shaping what happened next. Representation is easy to see from across the room; influence isn't.
Reflection
My own baseline Inventory already told me this: Dispositions is my strongest dimension. I don't need convincing that diversity matters — I already believe it and want it. That's a real strength to build from, even though believing it isn't the same as knowing how to notice or build it.
Yes — my baseline Inventory flagged exactly this gap: my dispositions are ahead of my knowledge and skills. I value diversity, but I haven't built much actual practice in auditing an environment for it, naming a monocultural norm out loud when I see one, or doing anything concrete to change it rather than just noticing it.

Audre Lorde's warning is worth keeping close: we often pour the energy it would take to explore difference into pretending it doesn't exist, or that it's an insurmountable wall — and either way, we lose the tool that real difference offers for creative change. Maya Angelou put the alternative simply: every thread in the tapestry is equal in value, whatever its color or texture.

A finding worth remembering: researchers Krishna Savani and Jared Nai found that people living in more racially diverse neighborhoods were more likely to identify with "all of humanity" rather than just their own group — and that this, in turn, predicted them actually being more likely to help a stranger. In one striking study, they traced 4,500 offers of help posted after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing and found more offers came from more diverse zip codes, even after controlling for distance from the bombing site.

SPHERE 4

Building Racial Empathy & Enhancing Racial Stamina

Key learning

Awareness here means understanding and sharing in the racial experiences of others without collapsing them into my own — empathy isn't replacing someone else's story with my closest personal analogy. Action means building the endurance to stay engaged over years, not weeks; this is the sphere the course guide names as where most equity work actually fails.

What stood out to me
Empathy done wrong replaces someone else's story with my closest personal comparison — that's substitution, not empathy.
Stamina, not any single hard conversation, is the actual bottleneck — most equity work fails from attrition, not from one bad moment.
There's a real difference between discomfort worth staying in and danger worth leaving, and I don't always pause to tell which one I'm actually in.
Reflection
Naming the discomfort out loud to myself, the same practice from my anxiety chapter, and remembering that freezing in one moment doesn't mean I have to stay frozen for the rest of the conversation. Having one script ready — asking for clarification — gives me something to do besides go quiet.
Probably most often in teaching: assuming that because I've felt unprepared or anxious in a conversation about race, a student or colleague of color must feel something similar — when their actual experience of that same moment likely isn't comparable to mine at all.
SPHERE 5

Acknowledging Racial Trauma & Fostering Racial Healing

Key learning

Awareness means naming and validating the trauma racism produces, including its physiological dimensions — this isn't metaphorical; racism has measurable effects on stress and health. Action means practicing pathways toward healing that are actually evidence-based and culturally grounded, not generic wellness language applied on top of a specific harm.

What stood out to me
Racism's effects on the body are measurable, not just emotional — that's not a metaphor, which changes how seriously I take "just let it go" advice.
Healing has to be culturally grounded, not generic — a wellness technique that ignores the specific harm isn't actually addressing it.
Acknowledging trauma is a separate step from fixing anything — rushing to solutions before naming the harm can itself be a kind of dismissal.
Reflection
Probably starting with asking the people actually affected what repair would look like to them, rather than importing a generic format and assuming it transfers. In a classroom, that might mean making space for the conversation rather than moving straight to the next unit once something difficult comes up.
The diversity-statement story from my previous employer is the clearest example — the suggestion got raised, met with confusion, and then quietly closed, with no one circling back to repair the gap it revealed. I didn't push it further either, which was its own version of treating it as something to just move past.
SPHERE 6

Gauging Racial Inequities & Championing Racial Justice

Key learning

Awareness means measuring and documenting racial inequities with enough rigor that a claim can actually be defended with evidence, not just a feeling that something's unequal. Action means championing the specific policy or practice that would move the number I measured — not stopping at just raising awareness of the number itself.

What stood out to me
Measuring something is itself a form of accountability — an inequity that's never measured is very easy to deny exists.
Championing justice means pushing for the specific policy that moves the number, not stopping at raising awareness of the number itself.
Evidence and emotion aren't opposites here — rigor makes the claim defensible, it doesn't replace the reason I cared about it in the first place.
Reflection
In course design, I could actually track whether outcomes or engagement differ depending on whose examples and case studies show up versus whose don't — instead of assuming equal materials automatically produce equal outcomes.
Something as simple as a standing review step before any course ships — checking who's represented in the examples and assessments before calling the material finished, instead of relying on someone noticing after the fact.

Equity and learning design

The ideas explored through RECI influence:

  • Accessibility
  • Inclusive course design
  • Learner engagement
  • Representation in course materials
  • Assessment practices
  • Communication and belonging
Reflection
A few concrete starting points rather than vague intentions: audit the examples and case studies in my course materials for whose names, contexts, and experiences show up by default, and diversify them deliberately rather than as a one-time fix. Examine my assessment methods for whether they quietly privilege one communication style or cultural default as the "right" way to demonstrate mastery — the same monocultural-norms question I was asking about workplaces applies just as much to a rubric. And stop treating accessibility and equity as two separate checklists, since they're really the same question asked twice: who was this designed for, and who wasn't.

Teaching the Next Generation

talking to my own kids about race, even when I'm not sure how

From MomsRising & EmbraceRace's 10 Tips for Teaching and Talking to Kids about Race — this one isn't hypothetical for me. As a parent, this is a guide I want to actually keep returning to, not just a thought experiment about someone else's kids.

Reflection
My parents, first — they tried to do better than the generation before them, even if what they modeled was still shaped by the limits of their own upbringing. But most of what I actually learned came later, once I got to college, where I had access to people, ideas, and experiences my parents' generation didn't. The gap between the two says something real: good intentions at home got me started, but it took leaving that environment to actually build understanding.
I want to keep learning how to actually help the next generation — not just avoid saying the wrong thing, but build real skill at this, the same way I'm trying to build skill everywhere else in this zine. My kids are going to learn about race from watching me whether or not I feel ready, so I'd rather be actively working on it than waiting until I feel qualified.
RESOURCES

Readings That Stood Out

A few pieces from the cohort's resources that I want to carry with me — and what they mean to me, in my own words.

Racial Anxiety — Rachel D. Godsil & L. Song Richardson

Argues that unwarranted racial disparities can arise from the anxiety both sides bring into a cross-racial interaction — not just from bias.

What stuck with me is that racial anxiety is its own mechanism, separate from bias. That reframes my own anxiety chapter: when I freeze in a conversation about race, it isn't automatic proof that I'm hiding some prejudice — it's a distinct, learnable pattern I can work on directly, the same way the article's ideas about scripts and the malleability of prejudice suggest.

Systemic Racism: Individuals and Interactions, Institutions and Society — Mahzarin R. Banaji, Susan T. Fiske & Douglas S. Massey

Traces how racism is "inbuilt" across four levels at once — institutional, societal, individual, and interactional — and why that makes it so hard to see from inside.

This is the piece that gave me language for what I noticed at my previous employer: the lack of a diversity policy wasn't any one person's personal failing, it was an institutional gap that let ordinary indifference go unchecked. "Good people" with no explicit racist intent were still the ones who let the diversity-statement suggestion quietly disappear — that's the article's whole argument, and it's an uncomfortable one to actually sit with rather than just agree with in theory.

The Loud Silence of Racism: It is Killing Us All — Bridgette L. Jones

A physician's personal account of the cost of staying silent about racism in professional spaces, and why remaining silent is itself a choice with consequences.

This one hit closest to home of anything I read. Jones writes about the real cost of staying silent in professional spaces, and that's exactly the pattern my own anxiety worksheet surfaced — I default to freezing rather than speaking, especially in teaching. Her point that silence isn't neutral, that it's a choice with its own consequences, is the line I keep coming back to.

Implicit Bias and Structural Inequity — Kathleen Osta & Hugh Vasquez, National Equity Project

Insists that implicit bias work only matters if it's tied to the structures that primed the bias in the first place — otherwise awareness becomes its own dead end.

This reframed implicit bias for me — it's not a personal flaw to confess and move past, it's downstream of real conditions (segregation, school tracking, media) that primed it in the first place. That means the fix isn't just "be more aware," it's actually changing the structures that keep priming people the same way. Individual awareness without structural change is, as the authors put it, its own dead end.

The Four "I"s of Oppression — TREC Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Resource

A compact framework for how oppression operates at the ideological, internalized, interpersonal, and institutional level simultaneously, each with its own antidote.

This gave me a vocabulary for something I'd only felt vaguely before: that oppression isn't one thing, it's four mechanisms reinforcing each other. At my previous employer, the Institutional gap (no policy) and the Interpersonal hesitation (a suggestion met with confusion, and no one pushing further) were both operating at once. Naming which "I" I'm actually looking at makes it easier to know what kind of fix is actually needed, instead of treating every problem the same way.

The Anti-Racist Resource Guide — Victoria Lynn Alexander

Practical scripts for interrupting bias in real conversations; also includes responses to common pushback lines, which I'm treating as a starting point rather than the final word.

The value of this guide for me wasn't any single script — it was realizing I don't need a perfect, original response ready before I'm allowed to speak. "Ask for clarification" especially gave me permission to respond with a question instead of freezing while I wait to have the fully-formed, correct thing to say. That's a small shift, but it directly targets my own default pattern.

Are People Kinder in Racially Diverse Neighborhoods? — Tom Jacobs, Pacific Standard

Covers Savani & Nai's research linking racially diverse neighborhoods to a stronger sense of identifying with "all of humanity" — and more actual helping behavior.

This gave me a concrete, research-backed reason to want diversity beyond fairness alone: homogeneous environments don't just lack representation, they measurably produce less helping behavior. That reframes my previous employer's lack of diversity as a practical loss, not only an ethical one — it was probably also a less generous place, in a way no one there ever had reason to notice or measure.

10 Tips for Teaching and Talking to Kids about Race — MomsRising & EmbraceRace

Practical guidance for raising racially literate kids — and a mirror for how I was (or wasn't) taught myself.

This made me think about the gap between my parents' effort and what actually got through to me. They tried to do better than their own upbringing, but these tips point at something specific I don't think happened much at home: questions being genuinely explored rather than gently redirected. If I'm ever in a position to do this for a kid, starting earlier and treating curiosity as welcome rather than awkward is the concrete change I'd want to make.
The Master's Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master's House — Audre Lorde
Lorde's argument is that reforming a system using the same tools and categories that built it will only ever produce limited change — real transformation needs different tools, not just a more inclusive use of the old ones. That reframes a lot of what's in this zine: a diversity statement is one of the master's tools. It's a start. It isn't the house.
